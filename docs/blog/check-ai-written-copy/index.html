Check What the AI Wrote Before Your Customers Do

A writing tool can turn out a page about your business in seconds, and it will read well. Where the tool did not know a fact, it may have supplied one anyway.

By Greg Quinn, owner, Corcoran Communications · October 7, 2026

Say a heating contractor in Perkasie asks a writing tool for an About page. A few seconds later there is one, and it reads beautifully. It says the company has served Bucks County for over twenty years, offers 24-hour emergency service, and is proud of its convenient location in downtown Quakertown.

The owner opened in 2015, does not answer the phone at night, and works out of Perkasie. Every one of those sentences is wrong, and not one of them looks wrong. That is the trouble with copy a machine writes: it fails in exactly the places a quick read does not catch.

Fluent is not the same as true

A writing tool is very good at sentences. It knows what an About page for a heating contractor usually says, and it writes that. What it cannot know is anything about your business you did not tell it: the year you opened, the towns you drive to, the hours somebody picks up the phone.

When it reaches a gap like that, it does not always leave a blank. It can fill the gap with whatever is typical, in the same confident voice as everything else on the page. A made-up claim and a true one look identical, so the only person who can tell them apart is the one who knows the business.

Wrong copy costs calls, not just credibility

A wrong fact on your website is not a typo. A customer who reads "24-hour emergency service" and calls at eleven at night was promised something, and the voicemail breaks the promise. A page that names towns you do not serve brings calls you have to turn away, while the towns you do serve go unmentioned.

A customer who catches one wrong detail also starts doubting the rest. If the address is off, why believe the warranty? That doubt rarely turns into a complaint. It turns into a call to somebody else.

Four things to check before it goes live

Every number.

Years in business, jobs completed, customers served, star ratings, response times. Every number in an AI draft is either one you gave it or one it made up, and you are the only one who knows which. If you cannot point to where a number came from, it comes out.

Every place name.

Towns, neighborhoods, streets, and regions are where a tool working from general knowledge slips, and locals notice in a second. Quakertown's downtown is West Broad Street, not Main Street. Souderton and Harleysville are the Indian Valley, while North Penn means Lansdale, North Wales, and Hatfield. A page that mixes those up reads as if it was written from a map.

Every promise.

Free estimates, same-day service, licensed and insured, satisfaction guaranteed. These phrases fill the typical page in your trade, which is exactly why a tool reaches for them. Each one is also something a customer will hold you to, so keep only the ones you would put in writing on an invoice.

Every service.

Ask a tool to describe a collision shop and it will list what collision shops usually do. Yours may not do glass, frame work, or fleet accounts. A service you do not offer brings calls you cannot take, and it pushes the work you actually want further down the page.

How to check a draft in one sitting

  • Give the tool your facts before you ask it to write: the year you opened, the towns you serve, your hours, and what you do and do not sell. The less it has to guess, the less it fills in.
  • Print the draft and underline every claim. Each number, place, promise, and service gets its own line.
  • Next to each underline, write where the fact comes from: your license, your records, your own knowledge. Anything left blank gets cut or rewritten.
  • Hand the page to whoever answers your phone. They hear what customers ask every day, and they will spot a promise you cannot keep faster than anyone.

None of that needs software or a consultant. It needs the person who knows the business and a pen.

Small towns read closely

In upper Bucks, a customer reading your page often knows the roads better than the tool that wrote it. Somebody from Sellersville will notice a shop placed on the wrong street, and somebody from Harleysville will notice their town filed under the wrong region. In Allentown or Bethlehem the same error is easier to miss, which only means it costs you quietly instead of out loud.

The details a tool gets wrong are the same details that tell a local reader you are actually from here. Getting them right is cheap, and it is the part of the page no competitor can copy.

The honest part

A writing tool is a reasonable way to get past a blank page. What it produces is a starting point, though, and the only fact-checker it can have is somebody who knows the business. Google's own guidance on AI-generated content says much the same: review it by hand for accuracy before it is published. [CONFIRM]

Judge the page by the numbers that pay a wage: phone calls, form fills, and booked jobs. A wrong promise can still bring in the call. It just cannot turn it into the job.

Questions, Answered

THREE THINGS OWNERS ASK ABOUT AI-WRITTEN COPY

Is it OK to use AI to write my website?

Using AI to write your website is fine as long as someone who knows the business checks every fact before it goes live. The tool is quick with sentences and has no way of knowing your hours, your service area, or the year you opened. Treat what it writes as a first draft, never as the finished page.

What does AI get wrong on small business websites?

AI writing tools are most likely to get wrong the details only the business knows: years in business, the towns it serves, the services it offers, and promises like free estimates or emergency service. Where the tool has a gap, it can fill it with what is typical for similar businesses. That is why the mistakes sound plausible and survive a quick read.

How do I fact-check a page an AI tool wrote?

To fact-check a page an AI tool wrote, print it and underline every number, place name, promise, and service in it. Next to each one, write where the fact comes from, such as your license or your own records, and cut or rewrite anything left blank. Then have whoever answers your phone read it, because they hear what customers ask every day.
