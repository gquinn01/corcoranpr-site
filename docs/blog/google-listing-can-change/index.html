Your Google Listing Can Change Without You

The hours, phone number, and address on your Google listing are not only yours to change. Other people can suggest edits, and a wrong closing time costs you the call before you ever hear about it.

By Greg Quinn, owner, Corcoran Communications · September 10, 2026

Somebody in Perkasie needs a windshield replaced, searches on a phone, and finds a shop in Quakertown that closes at six. They drive over after work, park on West Broad Street, and find the door locked. The shop closes at five, and always has.

Nobody calls to complain about that. They go somewhere else, and the shop never learns it happened. The hours that sent them there were wrong on Google, not on the shop's own website, and those are two different places. Only one of them is yours alone.

Your listing is a public record, not a form you filled in

A Google Business Profile looks like a page you own, because you claimed it and typed the details in yourself. It behaves more like a public record. Google gathers information about your business from many sources, and one of those sources is the public. A Maps user can suggest a correction to your hours, your phone number, your address, or your name, and Google decides what to publish without needing your approval first.

Most of those suggestions are well meant. That is what makes the wrong ones expensive: nobody is attacking you, so nothing looks like an attack. Anything that makes a suggestion easier to send means more of them arrive, which is why the answer here is a habit rather than a one-time fix. Google has begun rolling out a feature called Tell Maps in the United States, which lets a Maps contributor suggest an edit conversationally, even by uploading a photo of a sign.

Four fields, in the order they cost you money

The hours.

Hours are the most expensive field to get wrong, because a customer acts on them without ever contacting you. They also attract the most corrections, since anybody standing at your door at a quarter past five can see something you have not told Google. Holiday weeks and seasonal changes are worth entering before they arrive rather than after.

The phone number.

Call the number on your own listing once a month, from a phone that is not sitting on your desk. A wrong digit, a disconnected line, and a tracking number that expired all ring the same way, which is not at all. The listing looks perfectly healthy while it happens.

The address, and the town inside it.

Our own record is 1808 Enclave Dr, Quakertown, PA 18951, and that mailing address is correct exactly as written, even though the property itself sits in Milford Township. A helpful stranger correcting the town would put our listing out of step with every other place that address appears. Matching details across your listing, your website, and the directories is part of what tells Google a business is real, so an edit that improves one field can weaken the whole set.

The business name.

The name field takes your real business name and nothing else. Ours is Corcoran Communications, with no town and no service words added to it. Extra words look like an advantage, and they are the quickest way to make your listing disagree with your website, your invoices, and every directory you appear in.

The email nobody reads

Google sends notifications about your listing to whatever address is attached to the account. At a lot of small businesses that is a mailbox nobody opens: a former employee, a personal account from years back, or the web company that set the profile up and moved on. Those messages are the only warning you get before a change goes live.

So switching the notifications on is only half the job. The other half is making sure a person who works in the business reads that inbox every week, and knows the correct hours and phone number when a question about them arrives. Google has started sending owners a notification that shows a specific suggested edit and asks for a yes or no within four days. An alert nobody opens for a week can miss that window.

The five-minute check

  • Write down the correct hours, phone number, address, and business name once, and keep that record where you can find it. A check is a comparison, not a memory test.
  • Search your own business name on a phone and read the listing the way a customer sees it, not from inside the management screen.
  • Call the number on the listing.
  • Confirm the email on the account is one somebody in the building actually reads.
  • Review the photos and the questions on the listing. Both take contributions from the public as well.

Small towns feel it faster

In upper Bucks, and in the Indian Valley just over the county line, the people looking for what you sell in a given week are a countable group rather than a crowd. A shop in Sellersville or a restaurant in Souderton can lose a whole Saturday of calls to one wrong closing time, and a Saturday is a real share of that week. In Allentown the same mistake hides inside a bigger number, which is worse in its own way, because nobody notices it at all.

The listing is also the first thing most of those people see, and often the only thing. They read the hours, tap the number, and never reach your website at all. A site that took months to get right cannot rescue a closing time that is off by an hour.

The honest part

None of this is work you can buy your way out of once. A claimed and complete profile is where getting named by an AI assistant starts, and keeping it accurate is a habit one person has to own. Five minutes on a calendar every month beats any tool you could pay for.

Judge the listing by the numbers that pay a wage: how many people called, how many filled in the form, and how many of those turned into booked work. A listing showing the wrong closing time takes a bite out of all three, and it never sends you a note explaining why.

Questions, Answered

THREE THINGS OWNERS ASK ABOUT THEIR LISTING

Can someone else change my Google listing?

Someone else can suggest a change to your Google listing, including the hours, the phone number, the address, and the name, and Google decides what to publish. Most of those suggestions are well meant, which is what makes the wrong ones expensive. Your protection is a monthly check against a written record of the correct details, not a setting you switch on.

How often should I check my Google listing?

Check your Google listing once a month, and again before any week when your hours change, such as a holiday week. Monthly is frequent enough to catch a wrong edit before it has quietly cost you a season of phone calls, and short enough that it actually gets done. Five minutes covers the hours, the phone number, the address, and the name.

Does wrong information on my listing hurt my ranking?

Wrong information on your listing costs you customers before it costs you anything else, and that is the part worth fixing today. Hours that send somebody to a locked door lose the visit whatever your ranking is. Matching details across your listing, your website, and the directories you appear in is also part of what tells Google the business is real.
