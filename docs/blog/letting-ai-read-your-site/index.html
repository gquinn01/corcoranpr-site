Your Website May Be Telling AI to Stay Out

Most owners have never seen the file that tells AI assistants whether they are allowed to read the website. It takes thirty seconds to find, and nobody at your business necessarily chose what it says.

By Greg Quinn, owner, Corcoran Communications · September 16, 2026

Somebody in Sellersville needs a collision shop and never opens Google. They ask the assistant on their phone, in a sentence, the way they would ask a neighbor. Back comes an answer with two or three shops named in it and a line about each one.

To write that answer, something had to read those shops' websites. If yours said no, you were not a candidate. You did not rank badly and you did not lose on price. You were not in the running, and nothing on your end reports that.

There is a doorman file, and you can read it

Almost every website carries a small text file called robots.txt. It sits at the top level of the domain and it holds instructions for automated visitors: who may read the site, and which parts of it. It is closer to a sign on a door than a lock, and the large search and AI companies follow it.

You can look at yours in a browser. Type your own domain with /robots.txt on the end, so yourbusiness.com/robots.txt, and whatever comes back is what every automated visitor has been reading. A missing or empty file is fine, because it means nothing has been refused. If you want to see one that plainly says yes, ours is at corcoranpr.com/robots.txt, and it names every crawler we want in, one at a time.

Four names worth looking for

Two words do the work. Allow means come in, Disallow means stay out, and each applies to the User-agent named on the line above it. So you are reading for the word Disallow, and then for which of these it sits under.

GPTBot and OAI-SearchBot.

These two belong to OpenAI, and between them they are how anything in ChatGPT comes to know your site exists. If both are disallowed, an assistant answering a question about your trade has your business only from wherever else it appears.

ClaudeBot.

ClaudeBot is Anthropic's, behind Claude. The same rule holds, and for a small business the gap it leaves is wider than it sounds, because there may be very little written about you anywhere except your own pages.

PerplexityBot.

PerplexityBot is Perplexity's. An assistant with a smaller audience still gets asked who does this work locally, and still has to name somebody, so there is no version of this where being unreadable helps you.

Googlebot and Bingbot.

These two are ordinary search, not AI, and a site that refuses them is not in the results at all. That is a bigger problem than the AI question and it is worth ruling out in the same thirty seconds, because the symptom looks exactly like a marketing problem.

Nobody decided this

The words in that file were often never chosen. A site built on a platform or a content system usually has a setting, or a plugin, that writes robots.txt for you, and whatever the template said travels with it. A site that spent a few weeks on a staging address can still carry the blanket refusal that kept the unfinished version out of search. One article about AI companies training on other people's work is enough for a helpful person to paste in a block list and move on.

None of those are decisions about your business. They are settings that arrived. So the useful question is not who did this, it is whether what the file says today is what you would choose.

Refusing training and refusing answers are two different things

Some crawlers exist to gather material for training a model. Others exist to fetch a page so an assistant can answer a question about it. They often travel under separate names, and that distinction is worth knowing. It means an owner who objects to their writing becoming training material can refuse that and stay readable for answers.

Where a crawler's only job is training, refusing it costs you nothing in answers. Google-Extended and Applebot-Extended are controls of that kind rather than switches that make you findable, and Apple's own support documentation says that site rules for Applebot-Extended are not used in ranking for Apple's search. [CONFIRM: both halves of that sentence come from the Google Watcher's issue #26 rather than from anything published here. The Apple wording is quoted from support.apple.com/en-us/119829, and reading those two names as training controls rather than findability switches is the Watcher's.] The position worth holding is the specific one: refuse training if you want to, and leave the answer crawlers in.

The switch that is not on your website

Permission does not only live in a file. Search Console, Google's free dashboard for site owners, carries a control that takes a site out of Google's AI features. [CONFIRM: Google's rollout notice says this control reached all websites worldwide on August 31, 2026. We have that notice only as quoted by Search Engine Roundtable, and Google's own page would not open for us.] The part that outlasts any one setting is simpler. A person with access to your dashboard can take you off a surface without touching a single page, so knowing who holds that access is part of knowing what your site says.

Short lists have no room for the absent

Being unreadable costs more in Quakertown than it would in Philadelphia, and the reason is arithmetic. Ask an assistant for a general contractor in a city and it is cutting a long list down to three. Ask for one in Quakertown, Perkasie, or Coopersburg and there may only be a handful of real candidates before anybody starts choosing.

What fills your place is the business one town over. A customer in Quakertown will happily drive to Perkasie, so an assistant that cannot read your site names the Perkasie shop it can read, with no sense that it left anybody out. The phone call, the form fill, and the booked job all land there instead, and both shops carry on assuming nothing happened.

The thirty-second check

  1. Open your domain with /robots.txt on the end and read what comes back. You are looking for the word Disallow, and for any of the four names above.
  2. If you find Disallow followed by a bare slash, that is a refusal of the whole site to whichever visitor it names, and it is worth a call to whoever maintains your website this week.
  3. Write down who can change that file and who has access to your Search Console. If one of those is a company you stopped working with, that is the thing to fix first.

The honest part

Letting an assistant read your website does not get you named in its answer. It only makes being named possible, which is a floor and not a result. What moves the rest is a claimed Google listing, reviews with real words in them, being present where the answer is already looking, and pages that answer a question in plain sentences. That is the longer job.

Permission is the part you can settle today, by yourself, in less time than it took to read this. Not much else in local marketing works that way.

Questions, Answered

WHAT OWNERS ASK ABOUT LETTING AI READ A SITE

How do I check whether AI can read my website?

You can check whether AI can read your website by typing your own domain with /robots.txt on the end, so yourbusiness.com/robots.txt, and reading what comes back. The words that matter are Disallow and the crawler names GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ClaudeBot, and PerplexityBot. A file that is missing or empty is fine, because it means nothing has been refused.

Should a local business block AI crawlers?

A local business almost always wants AI crawlers in, because being named in an assistant's answer is how a customer who asked that assistant ever hears about you. Blocking them takes you out of those answers without taking you out of ordinary search results, so nothing in your reports explains where the phone calls went. If the objection is to your writing being used to train a model, that part can usually be refused on its own.

Does letting AI read my site mean it will recommend me?

Letting AI read your site makes being named possible, and does not by itself make it happen. Permission is the floor rather than the result. What moves the rest is a claimed Google listing, reviews with real words in them, being listed where the answer is already looking, and a website that answers questions in plain sentences.
