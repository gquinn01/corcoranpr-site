Should You Build a Page for Every Town You Serve?

Usually not. A town page works when you have something real to say about that town. Here's how to tell which towns deserve one, and how to get found in the rest.

By Greg Quinn, owner, Corcoran Communications · October 12, 2026

If you serve a lot of towns, somebody has probably told you to put up a page for each one. It sounds easy, and with an AI tool you could have twenty of them by lunchtime.

Most businesses shouldn't do it. A few good town pages will help you. Twenty that say the same thing can hurt you. Here's how to tell the difference.

Why a page for every town sounds smart

The idea makes sense. Someone in Perkasie needs a collision shop. They type the town into Google, or they search 'near me' and let Google decide. A page with Perkasie in the headline looks like a good match.

So the thinking goes: more towns, more pages, more chances. And if a page doesn't rank, no harm done.

That last part is where it goes wrong.

What goes wrong when the pages are all the same

When you write twenty town pages in an afternoon, you end up with one page copied twenty times. Only the town name changes. That causes two problems.

You have to keep every one of them up to date

Say your hours change, or you get a new phone number. Now you have twenty pages to fix, and some will get missed. A customer who lands on a page with the wrong closing time doesn't know it's an old page. They just show up to a locked door. It's the same thing that happens when your Google listing is wrong.

Google has said it can hurt your whole site

John Mueller, who speaks for Google Search, said in September 2026 that pages generated in bulk from combinations of names and attributes often produce a site that is spam, borderline spam, or low quality. He added that Google's systems can lose faith in the site as a whole, not only in those pages, and that recovering takes time and significant effort.

In plain terms, the copies can change how Google sees the rest of your site, including the pages you worked hard on.

What a good town page has on it

A town page is worth building when it tells the reader something true about that town and about your work there. Look for three things.

A local detail you'd only know from being there

Skip the population and the year the town was founded. Anybody can look those up. Use what locals know. Someone writing about Quakertown from another state would guess the main street is called Main Street. It's West Broad Street, and everyone who works in town knows that.

The work you really do there

What kinds of jobs come from that town? What do those customers ask about first? Put that on the page. Leave out any number you can't back up.

A question people in that town ask

Customers in different towns ask different things. If the questions on one town page are copied from the page for the next town over, a reader will notice. So will a search engine.

A quick test before you publish

Put the new page next to another page on your site and cover up the town names. If the two pages read the same, the new one isn't ready.

We ran this test on our own site in October 2026, and it caught us. Seven of our nine town pages had the same sentence about where our office is, with only the town name changed. We rewrote all seven. Each one now gives the real drive from that town to our office.

How to get found in a town without a page for it

You don't need a page for a town to show up there. Naming the town on a page you already have does a lot of the same work. A county page can cover a whole county. Our Bucks County page names Perkasie, Sellersville, Chalfont, and other towns as part of what it says about the county.

It also helps to use the names locals use. Around here, North Penn means Lansdale, North Wales, and Hatfield. Souderton and Harleysville are the Indian Valley. Get those right and the page sounds like it was written by someone who works here.

Where to start

Pick one town, other than your own, where you do the most work. Write that page yourself, or sit down with whoever builds your website and give them the details. Run the cover-up test, then publish it.

Do one page like that every few months. After a year you'll have four town pages you're proud of. That beats twenty you hope nobody reads closely.

Questions, Answered

THREE THINGS OWNERS ASK ABOUT TOWN PAGES

Should I have a separate page for every town I serve?

You usually shouldn't have a separate page for every town you serve, because most of those pages end up saying the same thing with a different town name. Build a page only for a town where you can say something true and specific about your work there. Cover the other towns by naming them on a page you already have.

How do I know if a town page is good enough to publish?

A town page is good enough to publish when it still reads differently from your other pages with the town names covered up. If two pages read the same once the names are hidden, the new one needs more that's specific to that town. A local detail, the work you really do there, and a question customers from that town ask are a good minimum.

Can customers find my business in a town I don't have a page for?

Customers can find your business in a town you have no page for, as long as that town is named on a page you already have. A county page or a service-area page that names the towns you cover does a lot of the same work. It also leaves you one page to keep up to date instead of twenty.
