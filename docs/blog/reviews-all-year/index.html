Ask for Reviews All Year, Not All at Once

A week of asking every customer at once builds a rating that looks good for a month and dated for a year. The version that keeps working is slower, smaller, and belongs to one person.

By Greg Quinn, owner, Corcoran Communications · September 23, 2026

A restaurant in Doylestown finally gets serious about reviews. The owner prints a card with a QR code on it, hands one to every table for a week, and watches the rating climb. Then the asking stops, because the week was the project and the project is finished.

A year later, the newest review on that listing is a year old. Everybody reading it is looking at a business that was good once.

A review is read with its date attached

Somebody deciding where to eat on a Friday night is not only counting stars. They are checking whether anyone has been in lately, the same way they check whether the photos look like the room they are about to walk into. A rating built entirely in one week two summers ago answers a question nobody asked. It says the place was good then.

A listing with one sudden cluster of reviews and nothing on either side of it has the same problem twice over. It reads as a push rather than as a business, and the customer scrolling those dates can see that as plainly as any software could. A steady trickle looks like what it is, which is people coming in and leaving happy all year.

What the habit actually looks like

None of this needs a budget or a tool. It needs one person who owns it and a sentence short enough to say out loud.

Ask when the work lands, not when you remember.

The right moment is the one where the customer is visibly pleased: the car looks right, the case is closed, the plates are cleared, the certificate is in hand. An hour later they are back in their day. A week later you are a stranger asking for a favor.

Make it one tap.

Put a direct link to the review box in a text message, on the invoice, and on the back of the card you already hand out. Every step you leave between the ask and the box costs you reviews from people who genuinely meant to write one.

Give it to one person, on one day a week.

A habit with no owner happens in bursts, because it only happens when somebody notices it has not been happening. Put it on one person and one repeating slot, whoever closes on Thursday or whoever sends the invoices out.

Answer every one of them.

Answer the good ones in a line and the harsh ones like a professional, without arguing. The reply is not really written for the person who left the review. It is for the next customer, who is reading the one star and watching what you do about it.

When the reviews go quiet

Sometimes the asking is happening and nothing arrives. Before assuming a link is broken or the website is at fault, open the mailbox attached to the Business Profile. It is the same inbox that warns you when somebody suggests a change to your hours, and at a lot of small businesses it is an address nobody has opened in years.

There is one cause worth knowing about before you go hunting for a bug. Google has emailed owners to say it detected a spike in spam reviews on their listing, removed those reviews, and paused new reviews on that profile for a few days. Where the reviews taken down were real ones, the owner can ask Google to look at the removal again rather than accepting it. [CONFIRM: both claims come from an owner's screenshot of the email, not from Google documentation. The trigger, the length of the pause, and how widely it is running are all unknown.]

A listing with almost no review history has the least for anyone to judge a new arrival against. That is its own argument for spreading the asking out from the first week rather than the tenth. A second location, a business that has just changed hands, and a profile that has sat untouched for two years are all in that position.

Upper Bucks feels it faster

In Quakertown, Sellersville, and the Indian Valley towns just over the county line, the people shopping for what you sell in a given week are a countable group rather than a crowd. A handful of recent reviews is a real share of everything a customer can find out about you. In Allentown or Bethlehem you are one listing among many, and the same handful moves you less, though it is still the difference between a call and a scroll past.

Who writes them matters here too. In a town this size the name on a review belongs to somebody your next customer may know, or know of, and the review itself mentions a street they can picture. That is worth more than any quantity of praise from nowhere in particular, and it is not available for purchase.

The honest part

A review habit is slow, and it is one of the few things in marketing that gets easier the longer you have been doing it. Nobody can front-load it for you. The week-long push is the version that feels like progress and then quietly turns into the problem it was meant to fix.

Judge it the way you judge everything else: how many people called, how many filled in the form, and how many of those turned into booked work. A listing whose newest review is a year old takes a bite out of all three, from people who never tell you they looked.

Questions, Answered

THREE THINGS OWNERS ASK ABOUT REVIEWS

Can I ask my customers for Google reviews?

You can ask your customers for Google reviews, and asking is ordinary practice rather than something to feel awkward about. What you cannot do is write them yourself, buy them, or fake them. Ask satisfied customers at the moment the work is finished, make it one tap, and take whatever they choose to write.

When is the best time to ask for a review?

The best time to ask for a review is the moment the work is finished and the customer is visibly happy with it. An hour later the feeling has faded, and a week later you are a stranger asking for a favor. Ask in person where you can, and follow up with one short message carrying a direct link.

Should I respond to a bad review?

Respond to a bad review, briefly and without arguing, because the reply is read by your next customer rather than by the person who wrote it. Answer every review you get, including the harsh ones, the way a professional would. What people judge is how you handle it, not whether your rating is perfect.
